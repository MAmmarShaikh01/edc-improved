Before Everything — Prerequisite

⚛ Understanding Electrons & Current

What is an Atom?

Everything around you — your phone, a wire, a piece of silicon — is made of atoms. An atom has three parts:

  • Nucleus — centre of the atom, contains protons (+) and neutrons (neutral)
  • Electrons — tiny negatively charged particles that orbit the nucleus in shells
Fig 0.1 — Basic atom structure. Outermost shell electrons are called Valence Electrons.
Valence Electrons — The electrons in the outermost shell of an atom. These are the ones that can break free and move, creating electricity. The fewer the valence electrons are held, the easier they move.

What is Electric Current?

When valence electrons break free and drift through a material, that movement of charge is called electric current.

⚡ Conventional Current vs. Electron Flow — The Most Important Distinction

Historically (before electrons were discovered), scientists assumed current flowed from positive (+) to negative (−). This is called Conventional Current.

In reality, electrons (negative charge) move from negative (−) to positive (+). This is called Electron Flow.

In this entire course, we use CONVENTIONAL CURRENT. So when you see an arrow in a circuit, it means conventional current goes in that direction (+ to −), even though electrons physically move the opposite way.

Fig 0.2 — Conventional current flows from + to −. Electrons physically move from − to +. We always use conventional current.
🧠 Mnemonic
"Conventional = Civilised (positive goes first, like a gentleman)"
In conventions (rules of society), positive people lead. Conventional current = + leads, goes to −. Electrons are rebels — they go the other way.
Current (I) = Charge (Q) / Time (t)  →  I = Q/t  [Amperes = Coulombs/second]

🔬 Materials Used in Electronics — Conductivity

The Three Types of Materials

Every material can be classified by how easily it allows electrons to flow through it. This is measured by conductivity (σ, sigma) — how well a material conducts electricity.

🧠 Mnemonic
"C-S-I like Crime Scene Investigation: Conductor, Semiconductor, Insulator"
Conductors = free to move (easy crime). Semiconductors = can be controlled (detective). Insulators = no movement (crime stopped).

Energy Band Theory — Why Materials Differ

Electrons exist in energy bands. Think of these like floors in a building:

  • Valence Band — The ground floor where electrons normally sit (bound to atoms)
  • Conduction Band — The floor above, where electrons are free to move (conduct electricity)
  • Band Gap — The gap between them; electrons must jump this gap to conduct
Fig 1.1 — Energy band diagram for conductors, semiconductors, and insulators

Semiconductors in Depth — Why They're Special

Semiconductors are the heart of all electronics. Pure silicon has 4 valence electrons and forms a covalent bond lattice with neighbouring atoms. At room temperature, a few electrons gain enough thermal energy to jump to the conduction band, leaving behind a hole (positive vacancy).

Hole — When an electron leaves its position in the valence band, it leaves a positive vacancy called a "hole." Holes move in the opposite direction to electrons. In conventional current, we track hole movement.
🌍 Real-Life Analogy

Holes moving through a lattice = Like a row of seats in a cinema where one person (electron) moves left. The empty seat (hole) appears to move right! The people didn't move right — but the empty space did. That's exactly how holes work.

Doping — Making Semiconductors Useful

Pure silicon (intrinsic semiconductor) has very few free carriers. We dope it — add tiny amounts of impurities — to control conductivity. This creates extrinsic semiconductors.

N-Type Semiconductor

Add an element with 5 valence electrons (like Phosphorus, Arsenic) to silicon (4 valence electrons). 4 electrons bond with silicon neighbors; the 5th is free to move. Majority carriers = electrons (negative).

🧠 Mnemonic: "N = Negative = extra electron = Nitrogen-like (5 valence) dopant"

P-Type Semiconductor

Add an element with 3 valence electrons (like Boron, Aluminium) to silicon. Only 3 bonds form, leaving one bond incomplete — a hole. Majority carriers = holes (positive).

🧠 Mnemonic: "P = Positive = hole = Boron (3 valence) dopant = DEFICIENT"

Fig 1.2 — N-type has a free electron (extra carrier); P-type has a hole (missing electron = positive vacancy)
Q: Why is silicon preferred over germanium in modern devices?
A: Silicon has a larger band gap (1.1 eV vs 0.66 eV for Ge), so it can work at higher temperatures without breakdown. It also forms a stable oxide (SiO₂) used as insulation in ICs. Silicon is also abundantly available (it's sand!). Real-life: your phone's processor is pure silicon technology.
Topic 2

💡 The Diode

What is a P-N Junction?

When a P-type and N-type semiconductor are joined together, they form a P-N junction — the basis of all diodes. At the junction, electrons from the N-side diffuse to fill holes on the P-side. This creates a region depleted of carriers called the depletion region, which acts like a barrier.

Built-in Potential (V₀) — The electric field created across the depletion region. For silicon: ~0.7V. For germanium: ~0.3V. This is the "barrier" that must be overcome before current flows.
Fig 2.1 — P-N Junction: depletion region forms at the boundary. Built-in field opposes further diffusion.

Diode Biasing — Forward and Reverse

Forward Bias (Diode ON)

Connect the positive terminal (+) of battery to the P-side (Anode) and negative to N-side. The battery pushes holes toward the junction and electrons toward the junction from the other side. When battery voltage > 0.7V (Si) or 0.3V (Ge), the barrier is overcome and large current flows.

Think of it like: the battery is a bulldozer pushing the "wall" (depletion region) until it breaks through.

Reverse Bias (Diode OFF)

Connect positive terminal (+) to N-side and negative to P-side. This widens the depletion region. Practically no current flows (only a tiny leakage current of microamperes).

The depletion region grows wider — the wall gets thicker and stronger.

Fig 2.2 — Diode in forward bias (current flows) and reverse bias (current blocked). Conventional current flows from Anode to Cathode.

The Diode I-V Characteristic Curve

The relationship between voltage and current in a diode is non-linear. This curve is one of the most important things to understand:

Fig 2.3 — Diode I-V curve. Exponential rise after 0.7V (Si). Tiny leakage in reverse. Breakdown at high reverse voltage.
🧠 Mnemonic — Diode Symbol
"Point to Positive, Block at Negative" (P-N = Positive-Negative = Anode-Cathode)
The triangle in ▶| points in the direction conventional current flows (from P/Anode to N/Cathode). The bar is the wall that stops reverse current.

Applications of Diodes

1. Half-Wave Rectifier

Converts AC (alternating current, which goes + and −) into pulsating DC (only positive half passes through). A single diode blocks the negative half of AC.

Fig 2.4 — Half-wave rectifier. Only positive half-cycles pass. The diode blocks negative cycles.

2. Full-Wave Bridge Rectifier

Uses 4 diodes arranged in a bridge. Both positive and negative halves of AC are converted to DC. More efficient than half-wave.

Fig 2.5 — Bridge rectifier: 4 diodes redirect both halves of AC to produce full-wave DC output.

3. Zener Diode (Voltage Regulator)

Zener Diode — A special diode designed to operate in the reverse breakdown region without being destroyed. It maintains a constant voltage across it regardless of current changes. Used as a voltage regulator.
🌍 Real-Life Example

Zener in phone charger: The 5V USB output stays constant even if you connect different devices drawing different amounts of current. A Zener diode (or its IC equivalent) holds the voltage steady like a pressure relief valve on a water tank.

🧠 Mnemonic — Diode Applications
"RLZS — Rectify, Limit, Zener-regulate, Switch"
R=Rectifier (AC→DC), L=Limiter/clipper, Z=Zener voltage regulator, S=Switch (logic gates)
Q: A silicon diode has a forward voltage drop of 0.7V. If 5V is applied and a 100Ω resistor is in series, find the current.
A: Voltage across resistor = 5V − 0.7V = 4.3V
Current I = V/R = 4.3V / 100Ω = 43 mA
Note: The 0.7V is "consumed" by the diode's barrier voltage. The rest drives current through the resistor.
Topic 3

🔌 Transistors — BJT and JFET

A transistor is a three-terminal semiconductor device. It can amplify (make small signals bigger) or switch (turn current on/off). It's the building block of every computer chip — billions of transistors in your phone's processor.

🌍 Real-Life Analogy

Transistor as a water valve: A tiny signal on the control terminal is like turning a small valve handle — which controls a massive flow of water. Small input → control of large output.

BJT — Bipolar Junction Transistor

BJT — A transistor with two P-N junctions. It has three terminals: Base (B), Collector (C), and Emitter (E). Called "bipolar" because it uses both electrons AND holes as charge carriers.

BJTs come in two types: NPN and PNP.

Fig 3.1 — NPN and PNP BJT structure and circuit symbols. The emitter arrow direction tells you the type.
🧠 Mnemonic — NPN vs PNP Arrow
NPN = "Not Pointing iN" | PNP = "Points iN Proudly"
In the circuit symbol, the emitter arrow for NPN points OUTWARD. For PNP it points INWARD (toward the base). Also remember: conventional current in NPN flows INTO the collector, OUT through emitter.

BJT Working Principle (NPN in Active Region)

In an NPN transistor, a small base current (I_B) controls a much larger collector current (I_C). The emitter current is the sum: I_E = I_B + I_C.

The transistor amplifies because of a parameter called β (beta) or h_FE — the current gain:

β = I_C / I_B   (typically 50–300)

So if β = 100 and I_B = 20μA → I_C = 100 × 20μA = 2mA. A tiny base current controls a 100× bigger collector current!

🌍 Real-Life Example

Microphone amplifier: Your voice creates a tiny current in a microphone (I_B-scale). This feeds the base of a BJT. The transistor's collector current, powered by a battery, is 100–300× larger — enough to drive a speaker. That's amplification!

BJT Configurations

BJTs can be wired in 3 configurations depending on which terminal is common (shared between input and output):

ConfigurationCommon TerminalCurrent GainVoltage GainUse
Common Emitter (CE)EmitterHigh (β)HighMost popular amplifier
Common Base (CB)Base<1HighHigh-frequency circuits
Common Collector (CC)CollectorHigh~1Buffer / impedance matching
🧠 Mnemonic
"CE = Current Excellent (both gains high). CB = Current Below 1 but Voltage high. CC = Current Collector, voltage stays same."

JFET — Junction Field Effect Transistor

JFET — A voltage-controlled device (unlike BJT which is current-controlled). A voltage at the Gate (G) controls the current flowing from Drain (D) to Source (S) through a channel. Only majority carriers flow (unipolar), so it's faster and has higher input impedance than BJT.

JFETs have two types: N-channel and P-channel.

Fig 3.3 — N-channel JFET structure and symbol. Negative VGS widens depletion region, narrowing the channel and reducing drain current.

JFET Working — The Pinch-Off Effect

In an N-channel JFET:

  • With VGS = 0: Channel is fully open. Maximum current (I_DSS) flows.
  • Apply negative VGS: Depletion regions from P-gates grow, narrowing the channel. Current decreases.
  • At VGS = VP (pinch-off voltage): Depletion regions meet, channel is fully pinched off. I_D ≈ 0.
I_D = I_DSS × (1 − VGS/VP)²  [Shockley's equation]
🌍 Real-Life Analogy

Garden hose with clamp: The N-channel is a hose. The Gate voltage is a clamp squeezing the hose. No clamp = full flow. Squeeze more = less flow. Full squeeze = no flow (pinched off). Unlike BJT, JFET gate draws nearly zero current (it's reverse biased), so it doesn't load the driving circuit.

Q: A BJT has β = 150 and I_B = 40 μA. Find I_C and I_E.
A:
I_C = β × I_B = 150 × 40μA = 6 mA
I_E = I_C + I_B = 6mA + 0.04mA = 6.04 mA
Note: I_E ≈ I_C since I_B is tiny. The transistor amplified a 40μA signal to 6mA — 150× amplification!
Topic 4 — Part A

📈 DC Analysis of Amplifier

When we analyze a transistor amplifier, we split the analysis into two parts: DC analysis (what are the steady-state voltages and currents with no signal?) and AC analysis (how does it handle the actual signal?). This is called superposition.

DC Analysis — Kill all AC sources (replace AC with open or short circuit depending on type). Find the steady DC currents and voltages — specifically the Q-point (quiescent operating point). Capacitors are open circuits for DC.

Voltage Divider Bias — DC Analysis Steps

For the CE amplifier (Fig 3.2), with voltage divider bias (R1, R2):

Step 1: Find V_B (Base Voltage)

V_B = VCC × R2 / (R1 + R2)

This is a simple voltage divider. R1 and R2 divide VCC to set the base voltage.

Example: VCC=12V, R1=10kΩ, R2=2.2kΩ → V_B = 12 × 2.2/(10+2.2) = 2.16V

Step 2: Find V_E (Emitter Voltage)

V_E = V_B − V_BE   (V_BE = 0.7V for silicon)

V_E = 2.16 − 0.7 = 1.46V

Step 3: Find I_E (Emitter Current)

I_E = V_E / RE

If RE = 1kΩ → I_E = 1.46V / 1kΩ = 1.46 mA

Step 4: Find I_C and V_CE

I_C ≈ I_E = 1.46 mA (since β >> 1)
V_CE = VCC − I_C(RC + RE)

If RC = 4.7kΩ → V_CE = 12 − 1.46mA × (4.7k + 1k) = 12 − 8.32 = 3.68V

The Q-point is at (V_CE = 3.68V, I_C = 1.46mA)

Topic 4 — Part B (Critical)

📐 Q-Point, Load Line & AC Analysis

What is the Q-Point?

Q-Point (Quiescent Operating Point) — The DC operating point of the transistor without any AC signal. It's the "resting position" of the transistor, defined by steady-state values of V_CE and I_C. The Q represents "quiescent" = quiet/still.
🌍 Real-Life Analogy

Q-point = A swing at rest. When a child sits still on a swing (no pushing), that's the Q-point. When you push it (AC signal), it swings symmetrically around that rest position. If the rest position is too low (swing dragging on ground) or too high (swing hits the top bar), the swing can't move freely — just like a badly biased transistor clips the signal.

The DC Load Line

The load line is a straight line drawn on the transistor's I_C vs V_CE characteristics. It represents all possible operating points given VCC and the circuit resistances. The equation comes from Kirchhoff's Voltage Law:

VCC = I_C × (RC + RE) + V_CE  →  I_C = (VCC − V_CE) / (RC + RE)

This is a linear equation (y = mx + c). Two easy points to plot it:

  • When I_C = 0: V_CE = VCC (x-intercept) — transistor is cut off
  • When V_CE = 0: I_C = VCC / (RC + RE) (y-intercept) — transistor saturated
Fig 5.1 — Load line drawn from saturation (y-axis) to cutoff (x-axis). Q-point is where the load line crosses the correct I_B curve. AC signal makes the operating point swing along the load line.

Role of Q-Point — Why Placement Matters

Ideal Q-point: Midpoint of Load Line

Placing Q at the center of the load line allows maximum undistorted AC signal swing — equal room above and below. This gives maximum output voltage swing (V_pp).

Fig 5.2 — Q-point placement effect. Too close to cutoff clips negative half; too close to saturation clips positive half. Midpoint = maximum undistorted swing.

AC Analysis — The Small-Signal Model

AC Analysis — Kill all DC sources (VCC → short circuit). Capacitors become short circuits (they pass AC freely). Find the amplifier's gain, input/output impedance for the signal.

Key parameter for AC analysis: r_e (dynamic emitter resistance)

r_e = 26mV / I_E   (at room temperature)

This is the intrinsic resistance that the AC signal "sees" at the emitter.

AC Voltage Gain of CE Amplifier

A_v = −RC / r_e   (negative = 180° phase inversion)

Example: If I_E = 1.46mA → r_e = 26mV/1.46mA = 17.8Ω. With RC = 4.7kΩ: A_v = −4700/17.8 = −264

The minus sign means the output is 180° out of phase with the input (when input goes up, output goes down). This is a key feature of the CE amplifier.

Fig 5.3 — CE amplifier output is amplified AND inverted (180° phase shift). Input goes up → output goes down.

AC Load Line vs DC Load Line

AC Load Line — In AC analysis, capacitors become shorts, so the AC "sees" RC in parallel with RL (load). The AC load line is steeper than the DC load line and passes through the same Q-point.
AC load = RC_ac = RC ∥ RL = (RC × RL) / (RC + RL)

The AC load line passes through the Q-point at a steeper slope (−1/RC_ac) compared to the DC load line slope (−1/(RC+RE)).

Q: Why must we remove RE from AC gain calculation but keep it in DC analysis?
A: In the circuit, a bypass capacitor (CE) is placed in parallel with RE. For DC, CE is open (capacitor blocks DC) so RE appears in the circuit and provides emitter degeneration for stability. For AC, CE is short circuit (capacitor passes AC), so RE is effectively bypassed and doesn't appear in AC gain. This is why r_e (not r_e + RE) appears in the AC gain formula. Without CE, gain = −RC/(r_e + RE) — much lower.
Q: What happens to the Q-point if temperature increases?
A: At higher temperature, V_BE decreases (~2mV/°C) and β increases. Both effects cause I_C to increase — Q-point shifts upward on the load line toward saturation. This can cause thermal runaway — more heat → more I_C → more heat. Emitter resistor RE stabilizes the Q-point by negative feedback: if I_C rises → I_E rises → V_E rises → V_BE = V_B − V_E decreases → I_B decreases → I_C decreases back.
